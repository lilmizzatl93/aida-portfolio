Case Study 03 · Scope Discipline & Process

Data Definitions

A foundational feature where I built the case for a disciplined MVP, catching cross-feature risks before they became problems.

Company
Precisely
Role
UX Designer → Sr. UX Designer
Timeline
2023
Status
Shipped, Audit Closed Jan 2025

A Note Before You Read This One

This isn't a clean-win story, and I'd rather be upfront about that

Data Definitions is a feature I'm honest with myself about - the shipped result doesn't fully reflect the design thinking that went into it. What follows is the range of exploration, the real technical decisions, and a scope-discipline story I'm genuinely proud of, alongside an honest account of where the process broke down. I think that's a more useful thing to show than a polished win would be.

The Feature

A technically dense, foundational capability

Data Definitions lets an internal persona connect a client's received JSON/XML data file to the platform, defining each field's name, type, format, and default so other parts of the system - tables, charts, personalized communications - can use that data. It's foundational: several other features depend on it working correctly.

Design Process

Real iteration, negotiated with engineering in good faith

Across three rounds of design check-ins, I worked through the full scope of the feature systematically - proposing simplifications, deferring things deliberately with reasons, and asking pointed technical questions instead of guessing.

Landing & Searching

Reusing an existing pattern to reduce new engineering surface area

Modeled search/filter behavior on the existing Customer Archive pattern, and proposed eliminating a redundant "exact match" search once better filtering was in place.

Editing & Safeguards

A technical safeguard built directly into the design

Designed a stable "Rapid ID" per data definition so edits wouldn't silently break existing connections, and worked out a nuanced used-vs-unused editing rule set with engineering.

Evidence Over Guessing

An engineering-sourced nesting-depth spec

Rather than picking an arbitrary limit, I asked engineering directly what the max practical nesting depth should be - a named engineer answered from real experience, and that number became the aligned spec.

Evidence-Based Decision-Making

Settling a contentious question with evidence, not opinion

Rather than deciding solo on whether users should be able to delete a data definition, I traced the actual downstream failure mode - a deleted definition already in use could break the active communication template's rules screen entirely, an orphaned record with no mapping back to what used it. I backed this with a real reproduced bug and gathered direct, attributed input from two senior stakeholders before the decision was made.

The Strongest Material

Forcing a data-grounded MVP decision when scope and timeline drifted apart

Partway through the project, scope and delivery timelines drifted out of alignment - things I'd already flagged as out-of-scope kept resurfacing as if they were still open, against a delivery date that had already been promised. Rather than keep re-litigating it informally, I built a comprehensive, function-by-function scope breakdown: every piece of functionality sized, estimated in sprints, and tagged as required or future enhancement - and critically, every single item I cut came paired with a concrete workaround, so nothing was simply dropped without an interim path.

I also traced where this feature's requirements would ripple into features that had already shipped - flagging a nesting-depth mismatch with an already-live dependent feature before it could surface as a production bug. That document became the basis for a cross-level working session - the right people in the room, at the right level - to drive a real, shared, data-grounded decision on what MVP actually meant.

Honest Outcome

What shipped, and what I still caught afterward

The version that shipped doesn't fully reflect the design intent captured above - real organizational constraints meant several planned pieces were cut without the follow-through they deserved. What I can point to with real pride: I personally closed the feature's later UX audit and caught a real inconsistency - a documented viewing limit that had silently changed from 250 to 1000 without design ever being informed - and pushed the fix to production myself. The paper trail on that catch is dated and exact, not reconstructed after the fact.
